Using a Rule Without Quoting It

If the words change, did the information still matter?

By Dean Ammons1 minute to readNovember 12, 2026
Read the exampleRelated research

The thought experiment explained

Give a model a newly generated rule, then ask it to apply that rule to a fresh problem. In a matched control, give the same model similarly formatted material that does not contain the rule. Keep the question and evaluation conditions fixed.

The question is whether the relevant exposure changes the distribution of meaningful answers. A model can use information without repeating the source’s wording. Measuring that use requires a declared task, a matched intervention, and a scoring rule that preserves meaning.

Paper I reports a small encounter-time pilot: 24 correct answers with relevant exposure and none with the matched control, across 12 paired microdomains. The two answers per context are not 24 independent experimental contexts. The result concerns one rule family with the rule present in context; it does not establish persistence after removal or historical training-source attribution.

Editorial explanation of Paper I’s operational question and encounter-time pilot; not a new canonical story. Paper I, Section 1, 3 and encounter-time pilot · DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24177478

What experiment would distinguish the use of a source’s information from a surface similarity in wording?

Influence is defined through a change in semantic response distributions under a specified exposure intervention. That measurement is separate from historical source attribution, persistent learning, or the rights attached to a work.

How the paper develops the question

A plain-language explanation of Paper I’s influence test.

The reported pilot uses 12 pairs, 24 contexts, one generated rule family, and two answers per context. The control lacks the queried rule. It tests in-context rule use; it does not validate a universal evidence-pooling law or demonstrate persistence after the source leaves the context.
